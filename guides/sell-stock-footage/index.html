Stock footage pays per licence, so income grows with the number of good clips you have online. Here's how it works and where the hours go.

The short version

  1. Upload clips to stock sites. Buyers license them and you earn a royalty each time.
  2. Video royalties run 15% to 40% depending on the site and your deal.
  3. Income grows with the number of good clips online, not with one great clip.
  4. Shoot what buyers search for, and start with footage you already own.
  5. The bottleneck is prep: trimming, grading, exporting and keywording every clip.

How stock footage pays.

A stock site licenses your clip to a buyer, often an editor, agency or brand. The buyer gets the right to use it. You keep the copyright, and the site pays you a share of the sale. The same clip can sell again and again, on several sites at once if you stay non-exclusive.

Income is a numbers game.

Sales are rarely spread evenly across a portfolio, and you can't tell in advance which clips will sell. So income comes from having many good clips online and adding more every month.

The math is simple, even if the inputs aren't:

clips online × sales per clip per year × your royalty per sale = yearly income

The sites don't publish average sales per clip, and anyone quoting one is guessing. What you control is the first number: how many sellable clips you get online, and how fast.

What buyers ask for.

Shoot to what the sites say buyers need, not what you'd like to film.

  • Evergreen themes. Adobe lists food, families, health and wellness, nature, and business topics like teamwork and the future of work.
  • Real people. Diverse, candid, authentic people, across ages and abilities. These need model releases.
  • Seasonal, early. Adobe suggests uploading seasonal content about 3 months before buyers need it.
  • Current briefs. Shutterstock's shot list asks for vertical video, video plates, sports fans, skilled trades and cultural holidays, among others.

Sources: Adobe, Create what's in demand, Shutterstock current briefs. Checked November 2026.

Start with footage you already own.

If you've shot for a few years, you probably have drives of b-roll, travel, drone and establishing shots. It's already paid for. Check the rights first (client contracts, releases, logos), then turn the best drives into clips.

The prep is where the hours go.

Every site wants the same thing: short clips, trimmed to one moment, graded, exported one per file, named and keyworded. Here are the steps by hand:

  1. Scrub each file and find the usable seconds.
  2. Set in and out so each clip fits the site's length.
  3. Grade log footage to a clean Rec 709.
  4. Export each clip as its own file.
  5. Rename it so you can find it again.
  6. Write a title and keywords for each site.

Our average is about 2 minutes per clip. That's why so many archives never get uploaded.

Your first 30 days.

  1. Week 1: sign up to Adobe Stock, Shutterstock and Pond5, and read each one's video rules.
  2. Week 1: sort your best drive into sell, editorial only and skip.
  3. Week 2: turn that drive into clips and upload your first batch.
  4. Week 3: read the rejections, fix the cause, and upload the next drive.
  5. Week 4: plan one new shoot around what the sites say buyers want.

What to know before you start.

Stock Shredder handles the repetitive parts. A few things stay with you.

  • You choose what to shoot. Demand decides sales more than any tool.
  • You review. It finds the usable shots. Your eye picks the ones that sell.
  • You own the rights check. Client contracts and releases are yours to confirm.
  • The prep stops being the limit. With clip prep handled, how much you shoot decides how fast the portfolio grows.

When by hand still makes sense.

  • You're uploading a few hero clips, each with a custom edit.
  • You're testing the waters with ten clips before deciding.

Once it's hundreds of clips, the prep decides how much of your footage ever earns. That's the part worth handing off.

FAQ

How does selling stock footage work?

You upload clips to stock sites. Buyers license a clip to use in their own work, and the site pays you a royalty, a share of what the buyer paid. You keep the copyright and can license the same clip again and again.

How much do stock sites pay for video?

Published video royalties in November 2026: Adobe Stock 35% of the net price, Pond5 40% exclusive or 30% non-exclusive, Shutterstock 15% to 40% by yearly level. Your income depends on how many clips you have online and how often they sell.

How many clips do I need to make money?

There's no fixed number, and anyone who promises one is guessing. Income scales with portfolio size and how well clips match what buyers search for. Portfolios grow by uploading steadily, month after month.

What camera do I need?

Most sites accept HD, and 4K is now common. Clean, steady, well-exposed footage matters more than the camera. Phone, drone and cinema camera footage all sell.

Can I use footage I already shot?

Yes, if you own the rights and have releases where people or private property are recognizable. Old drives are often the fastest way to start a portfolio.

What takes the most time?

Clip prep. Every clip has to be trimmed, graded, exported, named and keyworded. By hand that's about 2 minutes a clip in our tests.
