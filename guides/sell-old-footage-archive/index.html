The footage is paid for. The prep isn't.

Most working shooters sit on years of footage. Establishing shots from client jobs, drone flights from travel, the b-roll that never made the edit. It's already shot, so it costs nothing to sell. Except time.

Turning one long take into stock clips means scrubbing for the usable seconds, trimming, grading, exporting, naming and keywording each clip. At about 2 minutes a clip, 500 clips is 16+ hours. That's why most archives never get uploaded.

Here's how to get through yours without losing a month of evenings.

Step 1: check what you're allowed to sell.

Do this before you touch a single file. A clip you can't sell is wasted prep time.

  • Client contracts. Footage shot for a client may belong to the client, or come with limits on reuse. If the contract is unclear, ask.
  • People. Recognizable people need a signed model release for commercial use.
  • Places and property. Some private property and venues need a property release.
  • Logos and brands. Visible logos, brand names and artwork usually limit a clip to editorial use.
  • Editorial as a fallback. Newsworthy or documentary clips without releases can often go in as editorial, with a factual caption of what, where and when.

Each site publishes its own rules. Our stock footage requirements guide covers Adobe Stock, Pond5 and Shutterstock side by side.

Step 2: sort one drive into three piles.

  1. Sell. Your own shoots, nature, cities, drone, textures, anything with no people or with releases.
  2. Editorial only. Events, street scenes, anything with logos or unreleased people.
  3. Skip. Client-owned work, interviews, anything shaky or soft all the way through.

Start with the drive that has the most "sell" footage. Early sales tell you what's worth the next drive.

Step 3: turn long takes into clips, the slow part.

  1. Scrub each file for the usable seconds.
  2. Set in and out to a stock length.
  3. Grade log footage to a clean Rec 709.
  4. Export each clip as its own file.
  5. Rename it so you can find it again.
  6. Write a title and keywords.

Our average is about 2 minutes per finished clip. A drive with 500 good moments is two full work weeks of evenings.

Step 3, the Stock Shredder way.

Point Stock Shredder at a whole drive or folder on your Mac or Windows computer.

  • It finds the moments. Usable shots come out of long takes as clips, cut where the camera is calm, inside the Min and Max length you set.
  • It sets aside the bad parts. Broken, empty and too-shaky clips are filtered, with Keep anyway if you disagree.
  • It checks. Logos, HDR, wrong frame rates and footage under 2K are flagged before you upload. Handy for sorting sell from editorial.
  • It grades and exports. Built-in Looks for ARRI, DJI, RED and Sony, or your own LUT. Each clip its own ProRes file.

  • It never makes you review a clip twice. It remembers every clip you've reviewed, so you can work through an archive over weeks.

Step 4: one drive a week.

  • Monday: sort the drive into sell, editorial and skip.
  • Overnight: let the clip prep and export run. Stock Shredder keeps the computer awake and tells you when it's done.
  • Midweek: review the clips, then upload with the keywords already written.
  • Weekend: note what sold, and pick next week's drive by it.

Uploading to more than one site gives every clip more chances. See what each site requires before you start.

What to know before you start.

Stock Shredder handles the repetitive parts. A few things stay with you.

  • You own the rights check. No software can read your client contracts or know who signed a release.
  • You review. It finds the usable shots. Your eye picks the ones that sell.
  • Check your format. Canon Cinema RAW Light and Nikon N-RAW aren't supported yet. Sony, DJI, ARRI, RED and Blackmagic files are.
  • It runs overnight. A whole drive can be shredded and exported while you sleep, so the archive moves every week.

When by hand still makes sense.

  • Your archive is a few dozen clips, not a few drives.
  • Every clip needs a custom edit or a hero grade.
  • Most of the footage is client-owned or needs releases you don't have.

For drives of your own footage, the prep is the only thing between you and a portfolio. That's the part worth handing off.

FAQ

Can I sell footage I shot for a client?

Only if your contract lets you. Many contracts give the client the footage or limit how you can reuse it. Read yours first, and ask the client when it isn't clear.

Do I need releases for old footage?

For commercial use, stock sites ask for a model release when people are recognizable and a property release for some private places. Footage without them can often still go in as editorial, with an editorial caption.

How much of an old drive is usable?

It depends on how you shot. Long takes on a tripod, gimbal or drone usually hold several stock-length moments each. Run-and-gun interviews hold fewer. Sort one drive first and you'll know your own number.

Do I have to grade everything again?

Most sites take graded footage, and buyers usually want it ready to use. Log footage needs a grade before upload. Stock Shredder applies a built-in Look for ARRI, DJI, RED and Sony, or your own LUT, at export.

How long does it take to turn an archive into clips?

By hand, about 2 minutes per finished clip in our tests, so 1,000 clips is 33+ hours. Stock Shredder did the same 1,000 clips in 3 hours 1 minute, review not included.
