The short version

  1. Split your gear into kits: camera, lenses, power, media, monitoring, sound, grip.
  2. List every item on its own line. Count the ones you carry several of.
  3. Number look-alike items: batteries, cards, drives.
  4. Check the list when you pack, and again at wrap.
  5. Do the wrap check before the cases close, not in the truck.

Wrap is where gear goes missing.

Packing for a shoot is calm. You have the list, the time and a clear table. Wrap is the opposite: a dark stage, cases everywhere, and a truck that wants to go.

That is when the 75mm stays on the table, a charged battery sits on a stand, or a card stays in a reader. You find out on the next shoot. A good checklist works at both ends of the day, and it has to be fast enough to use when everyone is tired.

Below is our full list, kit by kit. Want it as a printable sheet with counts? Our free camera gear checklist maker builds one for your shoot type. Take what fits your shoots and drop the rest. A one-person documentary bag and a two-camera commercial package need very different lists, but they break down the same way.

Camera package.

  • Camera body, with body cap.
  • Top handle and side handle.
  • Cage or rig plate, with the screws and the hex key that fits them.
  • Quick release plate for your tripod head. The one that always gets left on the tripod.
  • Shoulder rig or baseplate and rods, if you use them.
  • Follow focus, with its gear rings or lens gears.
  • Matte box or lens hood, with flags and the right back.
  • Spare body, if the job needs one.

Lenses and filters.

  • Every lens on the job, each on its own line, with front and rear caps.
  • Lens adapter, if any lens needs one.
  • ND filters, or a variable ND.
  • Polarizer and any diffusion filters.
  • Lens cloth, blower and lens tissue.

Write each lens by focal length, not "lens set". A set is easy to tick off with one lens still on the table.

Power.

  • Camera batteries, numbered, with a count. Example: V-mount 1 to 4.
  • Battery chargers and their power cables.
  • Small batteries for monitors, lights and wireless.
  • AA and AAA batteries for sound and accessories.
  • D-tap and USB power cables.
  • Power strip and extension cord.
  • AC adapter for the camera, if you have one.

Media and data.

  • Media cards, numbered, with a count.
  • Card reader and its cable.
  • Backup drives, at least two, numbered.
  • Laptop and charger, if you offload on set.
  • Card wallet or case, with a way to mark full cards from empty ones.

Monitoring and video.

  • On-camera monitor, with mount, sunhood and power.
  • Director's monitor and stand, if the job has one.
  • Wireless video transmitter and receiver, as a pair.
  • SDI and HDMI cables, short and long, with a count.
  • Articulating arms and mounting screws.

Support.

  • Tripod and head.
  • Slider, gimbal or dolly, with batteries and plates.
  • Monopod or easy rig, if you use one.
  • Apple boxes and sandbags.

Sound.

  • Recorder, or the camera's audio module.
  • Shotgun mic, shock mount, windscreen and furry.
  • Boom pole.
  • Wireless lav kits, transmitters and receivers as pairs, with mounts and tape.
  • XLR cables, with a count.
  • Headphones.
  • Slate or timecode box.

Grip and lighting.

  • Lights, each with its power supply and yoke.
  • Light stands and C-stands, with a count.
  • Softboxes, grids and diffusion.
  • Reflector or bounce.
  • Clamps: A-clamps, super clamps, with a count.
  • Gaffer tape and cable ties.

Tools and small things.

This is the kit that goes missing most, because each item is small and lives outside a case.

  • Hex keys and a small screwdriver set.
  • Multitool or knife.
  • Head torch or small flashlight.
  • Sharpies, camera tape and labels.
  • Rain cover for the camera.
  • Phone charger and power bank.
  • Gloves.
  • Release forms and the call sheet, if they're on you.

How to use the list on a shoot.

  1. Build kits. One list per case or bag: the camera package, the grip package, the sound bag. Short lists get checked. Long ones get skimmed.
  2. Number the look-alikes. Two identical batteries are the classic loss: one comes home, so the count feels right. Label them 1, 2, 3.
  3. Check when you pack. Before you leave for the job, check every kit against its list.
  4. Check at wrap. Before the cases close. Once the lid is shut and the case is in the truck, nobody opens it again. Our film production wrap checklist has the order to do it in, plus a free printable sheet.
  5. Mark rented gear. Note where it came from and when it goes back. Rental gear is easy to lose because it isn't on your usual list.

The paper way, and where it breaks.

A printed list on a clipboard works. The checklist maker prints one with a box for packing and a box for wrap. It is free, it never runs out of battery and anyone can use it. Where it breaks is at wrap: in a dark room, with ten cases open, ticking boxes one by one is the step people skip.

A spreadsheet has the same problem. It is great for keeping the list, and hard to use with a battery in one hand and a phone in the other.

The Loadout way.

Loadout is our iPhone app for the wrap check. You save each kit once, then check the open case with the camera instead of a pen.

  1. Add gear. Aim the ring at each item once. No tags, stickers or QR codes on your gear.
  2. Build kits. Group gear the way you pack it: a camera package, a grip package, a sound bag.
  3. Scan. Pan your iPhone across the open case. Each item the camera spots is marked Seen.
  4. Confirm. Look-alikes, like two identical batteries, each wait for your tap. Then tap All packed.
  5. Leave. Anything not seen stays on the missing list. The finished check is saved with the date and time.

It works with no signal, because scanning runs on the phone. You can also mark gear as rented with a return-by date, put every kit into one Wrap Day check, and export a kit's list as a PDF or a spreadsheet for your producer or your insurer.

What to know before you start.

Loadout handles the counting. A few things stay with you.

  • You have the last word. Seen is not packed. Only items you confirm count, so a false match never closes a case.
  • Buried items need a look. Anything hidden under other gear stays on the list until you see it, or check it by hand with one tap.
  • Dark cases need light. Loadout tells you when it's too dark, and one tap turns on the light.
  • iPhone for now. Android is coming soon. Your kits are saved on your phone and in your private iCloud.

When paper makes more sense.

If you shoot alone with one bag and pack it the same way every time, a laminated card in the lid may be all you need. The list above still helps: build it once, number your batteries and cards, and check it at wrap. Loadout is for when there are several cases, rented gear, or a list that changes with every job.

FAQ

What should be on a camera gear checklist?

Group it by kit: camera body and rig, lenses and filters, power, media, monitoring, cables, sound, grip and lighting, then tools and small items. List each piece on its own line, with a count for things you carry several of, like batteries and cards.

When should I check my camera gear?

Twice. Once when you pack before the shoot, and once at wrap before the cases are closed and loaded. Wrap is when most gear gets left behind, because everyone is tired and packing fast.

How do I keep track of identical items like batteries and cards?

Number them. Label each battery, card and drive 1, 2, 3 and write the count on your list. Then check each number, not just the total. In Loadout, look-alike items each need their own tap before they count as packed.

Is a paper checklist good enough?

For a small kit you pack the same way every time, yes. It gets harder with several cases, rented gear, or a list that changes from job to job. That is where a checklist on your phone helps.

Does Loadout work without signal on set?

Yes. Scanning and checking run on the iPhone, so it works on stages, in basements and on remote locations.
