The short version

  1. Cut every clip to 5 to 60 seconds. 10 to 20 is safe everywhere.
  2. Keep the native size and frame rate. Don't upscale.
  3. Export ProRes or H.264 in a MOV or MP4.
  4. Stay under 3.9 GB per file, so Adobe Stock takes it too.
  5. Give log a light Rec 709 grade.
  6. Remove the audio unless it adds to the shot.

Meet Adobe Stock's rules and the same file passes on Pond5 and Shutterstock. One export, not three.

Stock prep already takes too long. Don't do it three times.

At about 2 minutes a clip, 500 clips is 16+ hours of trimming, grading and exporting. Most shooters sell on more than one site, and each site has its own rule page.

Guess wrong on one rule and you're back in the export queue: a file over the size limit, a frame rate one site won't take, audio you forgot to strip.

The good news: the rules overlap almost completely. Export once to the strictest ones and one file works everywhere. Here they are side by side, from each site's own pages.

One export that works on all three.

Take the strictest line from each row and you get one recipe.

  • Length. 10 to 20 seconds, with a second or two of clean handle at each end.
  • Frame rate. Keep the camera's rate. If you shot 48, know that Adobe Stock won't take it.
  • Size. Native camera size. Adobe Stock asks you not to resize unless you shot 4K or above.
  • Codec. ProRes 422 or 422 HQ in a MOV. It's on every site's list.
  • File size. Under 3.9 GB. High bitrate ProRes at 4K gets big fast, so watch your longest clips.
  • Color. A clean Rec 709 grade on log. Nothing heavy, buyers grade it themselves.
  • Audio. Off, unless it's real sound that helps the shot. Never music.
  • File names. Plain letters, numbers and underscores. Pond5 doesn't allow spaces, dashes, accents or symbols in names.

What still gets clips rejected.

Specs are the easy part. These are the checks that catch people on review day.

  • Edits inside a clip. Pond5 wants one shot per file. Split montages into single clips.
  • Logos and brands. A visible logo can sink a commercial clip.
  • Shake and focus hunting. Especially at the start and end of the clip.
  • Upscaled footage. HD blown up to 4K shows, and it gets refused.
  • HDR files. Shutterstock flags a known HDR issue with H.265. Deliver SDR Rec 709 unless a site asks for HDR.
  • Missing releases. Recognizable people or private property need a release, or an editorial upload.

Doing it by hand.

In Premiere Pro, Resolve or Final Cut, every clip needs the same six steps:

  1. Find the moment and set an in and out, 10 to 20 seconds.
  2. Grade log to Rec 709.
  3. Check frame rate, size and logos against the table above.
  4. Export as its own ProRes file, audio off.
  5. Name it with a clean file name.
  6. Write a title and keywords.

Our average is about 2 minutes per clip. A 500-clip upload is 16+ hours of that. The full walk-through is in How to Turn Raw Footage Into Stock Clips.

Doing it with Stock Shredder.

Stock Shredder runs on your Mac or Windows computer and builds the checks into the export.

  • Clip length. Set a minimum and maximum length before you shred. Every clip lands inside it.
  • Stock checks. Flags logos, HDR, wrong frame rates and footage under 2K before you upload.
  • Grade. Saved Looks for ARRI, DJI, RED and Sony, or your own LUT.
  • Export. Each clip its own ProRes file, up to 8K. Graded, log, or both in one pass.

What to know before you start.

Stock Shredder handles the repetitive parts. A few things stay with you.

  • You review. It finds the usable shots. Your eye decides which ones are worth uploading.
  • You upload. Names and keywords are ready to paste into each site. Releases for people and property are still yours to attach.
  • It exports ProRes. Every site takes it. If you want smaller H.264 files, convert after export.
  • Check your format. Canon Cinema RAW Light and Nikon N-RAW aren't supported yet.

When by hand still makes sense.

  • You're uploading a few clips, not a few hundred.
  • A site wants a special delivery, like HDR or an alpha channel.
  • You want H.264 straight out of your editor.

For a full drive, the rules are the same on every clip. That's the part worth handing off.
